The garden hose dies a foot from the spigot

Pull the dead hoses out of any garage and run the postmortem: the split is never in the middle of the run. It sits within a foot of a coupling, right where the hose bends hardest against the spigot. The fabric of a hose almost never wears out. The fitting zone fails, and the sticker never mentions it, because hose shopping is done by length and price alone.

The two-season hose is priced to fail there. A fifteen dollar vinyl hose ships with thin stamped fittings and a cover that stiffens in the sun, and two years of spigot kinks crack it at the female coupling. Replace it five times in ten years and the ledger reads $75 per decade, $7.50 a year, to stay in the checkout line buying the same footage over and over.

The reinforced rubber hose runs about $45 with brass couplings and a strain relieved ferrule, and it is speced for a decade and change. Twelve years of service puts it at $3.75 a year, $37.50 a decade. The triple sticker version costs half as much per decade. You are not paying more for a hose, you are paying once instead of five times.

Winter is the fitting killer that is free to prevent. A fifty foot, five eighths inch hose holds about 0.8 gallons, six and a half pounds of water. Left charged on the spigot in November it freezes, ice expands roughly 9 percent with nowhere to go, and the wall splits at the first crimp. Unhook it and walk it out; it drains in under a minute, and the $45 hose sees a twelfth winter.

So buy the fitting, not the fabric: brass couplings, a replaceable washer, a ferrule that does not crush the wall it grips. The hose ledger sits with the household durability reviews at DurablePicks.

Written for the fittings that outlive the yard season.
